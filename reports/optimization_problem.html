00

Where the integration actually stands

The two levels are now one optimization. The sizing group and the five-phase trajectory share a single design space: vehicle properties flow sizing → trajectory, and the energy, state of charge and mass the trajectory integrates flow back as the objective and the storage constraints. The uncoupled studies remain runnable on purpose — they are what the coupled answer is checked against.

Status, stated plainly

The coupled problem builds, runs, and returns feasible designs — m_init_error lands on zero, so the trajectory really does start at the mass the sizer built, and every inequality is satisfied. But SLSQP has not once reached its KKT tolerance: every run so far exits on the iteration limit (713, 800 and 1019 iterations in three attempts).

Worse, a warm start from the uncoupled optimum landed on a worse objective (48.2 Wh) than a cold start (28.6 Wh), which says the coupled problem has multiple basins and the driver is wandering between them rather than descending. Treat the coupled numbers below as feasible designs, not as optima, until that is fixed — the likely remedies are a proper NLP solver (IPOPT/SNOPT via pyoptsparse) and better scaling, not more iterations.

Read this before using any trajectory number

build_mission_problem() takes S_ref, AR, C_D0, A_rotor, E_batt_wh and m_initial_kg as typed defaults, not as outputs of the sizing optimizer. So the trajectory currently flies a 20 kg aircraft with a 0.72 m² wing, while the sizer says the right answer is 9.54 kg with 0.312 m². Each result is self-consistent; they describe different vehicles.

Closing this is one wiring change — pass the sizing result into the trajectory builder — plus a re-validation of the trajectory at the smaller vehicle's much lower power levels. It is the next piece of work, and until it is done the two levels should be read separately.

What is integrated, as of this session: m1 terrain now drives both levels. Level 1 gets range, pad elevation and the ridge height that bounds cruise altitude; Level 2 gets a differentiable terrain(x) model that its AGL path constraint is evaluated against. Before, neither knew the ridge existed.

01

Level 1 — the statement

Static sizing MDO, in the standard form.

minimize f(x) = Emission(x) + λ · Σi wi(x)·(1 − wi(x)) λ = 200 Wh
by varying xiL ≤ xi ≤ xiU, i = 1 … 12
subject to gj(x) ≤ 0, j = 1 … 5
h(x) = 0 — the mass-closure loop, converged by NonlinearBlockGS rather than handed to the driver

The equality is worth a note. Mass closure — MTOW feeding geometry and structures, which feed back into mass — is a genuine algebraic loop. It could be posed as an equality constraint with MTOW as a design variable. Here it is instead converged inside each function evaluation by a Gauss–Seidel solver with Aitken relaxation, so the driver never sees it. That keeps the design space at 12 variables instead of 13, and it is why n_h = 0 at this level.

altitude became a design variable in this session. Its bounds come from the terrain: the lower bound is the departure pad, the upper is 1500 m above the clearance floor. It could not sensibly be a variable before, because nothing in the model knew what altitude was safe.

z_arch is the framework's novelty

Six real numbers pass through a softmax to become weights w = softmax(1.5·z), and every architecture-dependent quantity — propulsion mass, fuel flow, bus power, fuel LHV, fuel capability — is the weighted blend across all six architectures. Architecture choice becomes a gradient computation instead of six separate discrete runs. The penalty term λ Σ wᵢ(1−wᵢ) vanishes only when the weights are one-hot, which is what forces the answer back to a buildable machine.

It has one hazard worth stating: at z = 0 all six weights are 1/6 and the penalty gradient is exactly zero by symmetry. A relaxed run must be warm-started from each architecture in turn (--multistart) or it sits on that saddle forever.

05

The SOC gap — what it actually was

Not a missing constraint. A physics error in the energy accounting.

The previous run reported g2_energy_margin = −0.163 for all_electric — comfortably feasible. It was not. EnergyComp credited every design with usable fuel energy regardless of architecture:

before — no architecture in sight
usable_fuel = E_fuel_wh * eta * (1.0 - fuel_reserve)
energy_available_wh = usable_batt + usable_fuel

So the "optimal" all-electric aircraft carried 0.022 kg of gasoline and drew 48.9% of its available energy from a fuel it had no engine, turbine or fuel cell to burn. On battery alone the same design was 64% short of the energy its own mission required.

after — gated by what the architecture can actually do
# 0 for all_electric, 1 for anything with a fuel converter,
# blended by the same softmax weights as every other quantity.
_HAS_FUEL_PATH = np.array([0, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1])
fuel_capable   = np.sum(w * _HAS_FUEL_PATH)

k_eff       = 1.0 - fuel_capable * (1.0 - k_electric)   # battery takes it all
m_fuel      = fuel_capable * m_fuel                     # no dead fuel mass
usable_fuel = fuel_capable * E_fuel_wh * eta * (1.0 - fuel_reserve)
Why gating beats a hard switch

fuel_capable is a blended weight, not an if. A half-weighted architecture blend gets half the fuel capability, so the quantity stays differentiable and the relaxation can still move through it. A branch would have put a discontinuity in the middle of the design space the whole method depends on being smooth.

SOC_final and constraint g₃ were added alongside it. Being honest about g₃: the battery is sized to meet its energy requirement including the reserve, so g₃ is satisfied by construction rather than driven by the optimizer. Its job is to make the reserve auditable in every result instead of an implicit property of a formula buried in one component — and to fail loudly if a future change breaks the sizing rule.

All-electric wins on this 13.6 km corridor, and that is the expected result rather than a surprising one: at this range the hybrids carry a powerplant whose mass they never amortise. Terrain clearance in the trajectory

This was the second gap. Ground elevation had been one constant per phase — for cruise, the mean of the two pads, about 2600 m. On a route crossing a 3026 m ridge, a trajectory could satisfy a 100 m AGL path constraint while flying 400 m inside the mountain.

The DEM itself cannot go into the ODE: it is a gridded interpolator, and dymos needs something complex-step differentiable. So terrain is fitted as a sum of Gaussian bumps in ground distance — z(x) = Σⱼ wⱼ·exp(−((x − cⱼ)/σ)²) + lift — which exp handles on complex input natively.

The fit is deliberately conservative

A least-squares fit dips below a sharp ridge by construction, and under-predicting terrain is the one error that matters here — it would licence a trajectory to fly into rock. The whole surface is therefore lifted by the worst under-prediction, making it a verified upper bound everywhere. With 60 centres at σ = 453 m that costs a 15 m lift and 16 m RMS of extra caution. A polynomial basis needed 175 m of slack for the same guarantee, which is why it was abandoned.

Clearance is now a path constraint on every wingborne phase, not cruise alone — 48 constrained nodes against the previous 24. Cruise carries the full 100 m floor; the transitions carry the mission's 50 m minimum, because they are flown near the pads by design.

The solved trajectory

Converged in 536 iterations, all five phases, zero physics violations — 92.5 Wh, and 4×10⁻⁶ kg of fuel for an all-electric vehicle (the residue of the softmax's 0.0006 weight on the fuel architectures, not a fuel path).

Cruise altitude spans 2498 m to 3267 m — the trajectory climbs over the ridge and descends into the Cumbayá valley, tracking terrain rather than holding one altitude. With the old constant ground elevation this same trajectory would have been free to fly straight through the mountain.

08

What is left

  1. Couple the two levels. The single most valuable next step, and the direct answer to "what stage are we at". Pass the sizing optimum into build_mission_problem() so the trajectory flies the aircraft the sizer chose, then re-validate — the smaller vehicle's power levels are roughly half what the trajectory is currently set up for.
  2. g₃ does not yet drive anything. The battery is sized to meet its reserve, so the constraint is satisfied by construction. Making battery mass a design variable would turn g₃ into a real driver and let the optimizer trade battery mass against reserve properly.
  3. Bounds are binding. AR and wing_loading sit on their upper limits for all six architectures. Widen them, or justify them as manufacturing limits — but do not read the current values as an interior optimum.
  4. Shaft energy is not a fair cross-architecture objective. Comparing hybrids to a battery aircraft on shaft Wh charges the hybrids for conversion losses in a way that a stored-energy or mass-based objective would not.
  5. Two trajectory codepaths still exist. The CasADi OCP in m7_trajectory/ocp.py remains as an independent reference and has not been retired.
